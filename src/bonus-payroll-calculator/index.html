The same bonus costs three different amounts

Employer Social Security is charged only on the part of a bonus that is still below the employee’s 2026 Social Security wage base of $184,500, while employer Medicare is charged on the whole bonus because Medicare has no wage base limit (IRS Topic 751, checked November 24, 2026). The 0.9% Additional Medicare tax on wages above $200,000 has no employer match, so it does not change the employer cost below. That makes one $5,000 bonus cost three different amounts for three employees in the same year. The amounts are this page’s own arithmetic at the rates shown.

Employer Medicare is $72.50 in every case. Employer Social Security on the same $5,000 bonus falls from $310.00 to $0.00, a $310.00 difference, and that is the whole spread between the first row and the last. In the middle row the $5,000 bonus crosses the base, so only $4,500 of it is still subject to Social Security.

The $1,000 bonus behaves the same way: $1,000.00 + $62.00 + $14.50 = $1,076.50 below the base, and $1,000.00 + $0.00 + $14.50 = $1,014.50 above it.

Federal unemployment tax sits on its own line and is not part of that subtotal. At 0.6% on the first $7,000 of the employee’s 2026 wages when the employer is eligible for the full state credit, a $5,000 bonus adds $30.00 if the employee has not yet reached $7,000 of FUTA wages, $0.00 if they have, and a partial amount in between. A state’s credit reduction can raise the rate above 0.6%, so the amount has to come from the employer’s notice. FUTA is therefore entered as an amount rather than assumed as a rate on the employer payroll taxes page, which carries the annual employer-tax model.

A nondiscretionary bonus can cost more than the tax lines

A bonus is only excluded from the regular rate of pay if it is discretionary, and it is discretionary only if all three requirements are met: the employer has sole discretion, until at or near the end of the corresponding period, over whether to pay it; the employer has sole discretion over the amount; and it is not paid according to any prior contract, agreement or promise causing the employee to expect it regularly (DOL Fact Sheet #56C, fact sheet dated December 2019, checked November 24, 2026). The label assigned to the bonus and the reason for the bonus do not conclusively determine whether a bonus is discretionary. Nondiscretionary bonuses must be included in the regular rate of pay, and the fact sheet lists bonuses based on a predetermined formula such as production bonuses, quality or accuracy bonuses, bonuses announced to induce greater efficiency, attendance bonuses and safety bonuses among them.

Once a nondiscretionary bonus is in the regular rate, the regular rate is total compensation for the week (excluding statutory exclusions) divided by total hours worked, overtime already paid is recalculated and topped up, and a discretionary bonus may not be credited towards overtime compensation due under the FLSA. This applies only to a non-exempt employee, and only when the bonus is nondiscretionary. It also depends on hours the page cannot know, so it stays pending until the employer has the employee’s hours and the period the bonus covers. It is not zero.

Federal supplemental withholding is not the same as employer cost

For separately identified supplemental wages, IRS Publication 15 describes an optional 22% federal flat withholding method. It may be used only if the supplemental wage is identified separately from the regular wage payment and the employer withheld income tax on the employee’s regular wages on a regular basis during the year, or the employee had income tax withheld in the immediately preceding calendar year; where either condition is missing, the aggregate method applies (Publication 15, section 7, checked November 24, 2026). Publication 15 also states that an employer must withhold at the highest rate of 37% on any supplemental wage paid to an employee during the calendar year if the wages paid to that employee during the calendar year exceed $1 million.

That withholding is not the employee’s final tax, and it is not employer cost. It is a prepayment against the employee’s annual liability.

If the employer promises a net amount rather than a gross amount, the bonus has to be grossed up before the tax lines above apply. That calculation is on the gross-up calculator.

What to confirm before paying a bonus

  • Whether the payment is separately identified as supplemental wages.
  • Employee Social Security wage-base status and any Additional Medicare Tax handling.
  • Where this employee stands against the $184,500 Social Security base for 2026, and how much of the bonus is still below it.
  • Whether the $7,000 federal unemployment base is already used up for this employee in 2026.
  • Whether the bonus is discretionary or nondiscretionary, and which period it covers.
  • Where this employee stands against the state unemployment base.
  • FUTA, state unemployment, local taxes, workers’ compensation and benefit effects.
  • Whether the written payroll register shows gross bonus, employee withholding and employer cost as separate lines.

FAQ

Does employee withholding reduce employer bonus cost?

No. It reduces the employee’s payment; employer cost starts with gross bonus pay and adds applicable employer-side taxes and benefits.

Is $1,076.50 a guaranteed cost for a $1,000 bonus?

No. It uses the stated Social Security and Medicare fixture only, and it holds while this employee’s 2026 Social Security wages paid by this employer are below $184,500; above that base the same $1,000 bonus costs $1,014.50. FUTA, state and other items may change the result.

Does this calculate the employee’s final tax?

No. It separates an employer-cost fixture from federal supplemental withholding and final tax liability.

Does every $1,000 bonus cost $1,076.50?

No. $1,076.50 is the figure below the 2026 Social Security wage base, and $1,014.50 is the figure above it, for an employee whose Social Security wages at this employer have already passed $184,500. Federal unemployment, state unemployment, workers’ compensation, benefits and any overtime true-up are separate lines on top of either figure.

Does a bonus change overtime pay?

Yes, for a nondiscretionary bonus paid to a non-exempt employee, per DOL Fact Sheet #56C: the bonus goes into the regular rate and the overtime already paid is recalculated and topped up. A discretionary bonus does not, but the label alone does not decide which kind a bonus is.
