Use one employee's records. Enter durations, not clock-in times or decimal hours. Keep each actual rate on its own row. The tool adds exact minute × rate-cent numerators, divides their sum by 60, then rounds the final positive subtotal to the nearest cent, with a half-cent rounded up. It does not round punches. This is the calculation method used here, not a claim that every payroll system must use the same rounding convention.

Separate a time-format error from a rounding choice

A colon and a decimal point describe different units. In 7:30, the second part is thirty minutes. In 7.30 hours, it is thirty hundredths of an hour: 0.30 × 60 = 18 minutes. The missing 30 − 18 = 12 minutes explains the $4.00 difference at $20/hour: 12 ÷ 60 × $20 = $4.00. This is a unit error before any cent-rounding decision.

The 224-minute example is different. Its durations are valid, but converting each to only two decimal places loses precision before the wage multiplication. Retaining 224 minutes lets the tool carry the fractional cents until the final subtotal. The displayed dollar differences are original sensitivity examples, not evidence that a particular employer or another calculator underpays anyone.

For an audit, retain the underlying minutes, the rate and the rounding method, rather than only a screenshot of decimal hours. A different documented payroll convention can produce a different reconciliation. First reproduce that convention; do not silently adjust the recorded time to make the numbers match.

Multiple rates are not an overtime determination

The $204 example keeps two rates attached to two durations. Using one rate for every entry would erase information. But even a correct straight-time sum does not settle overtime. The federal regular-rate calculation can involve compensation beyond a single quoted hourly rate, and the employee's actual workweek matters. Coverage and exemptions must also be established.

The DOL official overtime general principles · checked October 27, 2026 explains the distinction. This tool does not classify employees, allocate overtime hours, calculate bonuses or premiums, or determine tax withholding. Leave those items open until reviewed; do not label this subtotal as net pay.

Three questions before using the subtotal

Is 7:30 the same as 7.30 hours?

No. 7:30 is seven hours thirty minutes, or 7.50 decimal hours. Enter hours and minutes in their separate fields so a decimal point cannot change their meaning.

Why can rounded decimal hours change the pay?

Rounding the time before multiplying can discard fractions that affect cents. The worked 224-minute example yields $74.67 at the illustrated rate, while the prematurely rounded 3.73-hour version yields $74.60.

Does this subtotal include overtime or taxes?

No. It sums straight-time amounts at the rates you entered. Overtime, additional compensation, withholding and deductions require separate review before a complete payroll or take-home figure can be stated.
